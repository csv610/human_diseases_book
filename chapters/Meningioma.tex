\section{Causes}
This condition results from disruption of normal meningioma function.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item This condition happens because of neoplastic growth of arachnoid cap cells. Most are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally on imaging
  \item Symptomatic meningiomas cause headaches, seizures, or focal deficits depending on location.
\end{itemize}

\section{Progression and Stages}
The condition may progress through different stages of severity over time. Early stages often involve mild or subtle symptoms that may be overlooked. Moderate stages involve more noticeable symptoms affecting daily function. Advanced stages may involve significant impairment and complications.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Meningioma is the most common primary intracranial tumor, arising from arachnoid cap cells
  \item It is usually non-cancerous (WHO grade 1), slow-growing, and attached to the dura
  \item Meningiomas are more common in women and associated with prior radiation, neurofibromatosis type 2, and high progesterone receptor expression. Treatment focuses on observation for small, asymptomatic lesions
  \item Surgical removal is the mainstay for symptomatic or growing tumors
  \item Atypical (grade 2) and anaplastic (grade 3) meningiomas require adjuvant radiotherapy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Diagnosis}
Doctors diagnose this based on MRI revealing a well-circumscribed, homogeneously enhancing dural-based mass with a dural tail. \section{Outlook}
Most people recover well for non-cancerous meningiomas. The outlook varies depending on the specific condition, severity at diagnosis, and response to treatment. Early intervention generally leads to better outcomes. Many conditions can be effectively managed with appropriate treatment. Regular follow-up care is important for monitoring and treatment adjustment.
